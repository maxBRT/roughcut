## Verdict on risk 5

**Retired, with one change to the settings and one change to the speed expectation.**

- **Method:** clip by clip, as spec 2.4.8 says. Every exactness check passed on H.264, HEVC Main10 HDR10, variable frame rate and 30000/1001 sources: exactly the expected frames in the expected order, the right frame on each side of every cut, output length equal to the sum of the clips to the frame, both audio tracks the same length as the video to within 0.005 ms, beeps within 0.67 ms of where they belong at the end of the file, regular packet timestamps through every join.
- **Default quality: constant quality 19, preset p4,** for both `h264_nvenc` and `hevc_nvenc`. On the hard (grainy, camera-like) half of the test source it gives VMAF 98.3 (H.264) and 99.9 (HEVC); the screen-like half is VMAF 100.
- **Settings change found by this run:** for H.264, B-frames must **not** be used as references (`-b_ref_mode disabled`). With `-b_ref_mode middle`, three of three full-length H.264 encodes that were checked decode differently in the processor decoder (libavcodec) and in NVDEC, for 16 to 55 frames at a time, down to 16 dB PSNR between the two decoders, once with a decoder error. Your editor's software has its own decoder, so a stream that decoders disagree on is not acceptable. With `disabled` the same encode decodes identically in both. Cost: 15 % larger files (541 instead of 472 MB per minute on the test source), slightly higher quality, same speed. HEVC keeps `middle`: its full-length encodes decoded identically apart from the sporadic GPU readback differences described under "Things that needed care", which also hit files with no B-frame references.
- **Speed:** a long clip encodes at about **4 times real time** (4.1 x H.264, 4.2 x HEVC 10-bit, 4K30, GPU otherwise idle). Every clip also costs about **1 second** of fixed work (process start, seek, encoder start). **Run two pieces at a time**: it cut the video time of 60 two-second clips from 63 to 35 s (HEVC) and of a 12-clip edit by about 20 %. A transcript edit with hundreds of clips is therefore about 3 times real time, not the 4 to 6 times that spec 2.4.8 and 2.6 estimate: about **13 to 14 minutes for a 41-minute result with 300 clips** two at a time, about 18 minutes one at a time (see "Export estimate").
- **Single pass rejected:** one ffmpeg run that decodes every clip as its own GPU input was as exact, and as fast as clip by clip with two pieces at a time (41.6 against 42.1 s for 12 clips), but it needed 5.9 to 10.9 GB of GPU memory for 12 clips (about 0.5 to 0.9 GB per clip), so it cannot run an edit of hundreds of clips. Clip by clip used 1.35 GB.

## 1. The method (export.py)

For an edit list of clips `(source, start_frame, frame_count)` on the sequence frame grid:

1. **Encode clip**: one ffmpeg process per clip. NVDEC decodes the original; a fast seek lands up to 1 s before the clip; the `fps` filter puts frames on the sequence grid (this is also what turns a variable-frame-rate source into constant frame rate); `trim` on frame slots cuts exactly `frame_count` frames; NVENC encodes. Every piece of one export has identical settings.
2. **Render audio**: one ffmpeg process renders every audio track for the whole sequence as 32-bit float PCM in a NUT file, with a 4 ms fade in and out at every clip edge.
3. **Join**: the concat demuxer joins the pieces with `-c:v copy`, the audio is encoded to AAC 256 kb/s per track, and the MP4 is written under a temporary name, then renamed.

The 12-clip edit (`run_exactness.EDIT`): out of source order, from two sources, clips of 1, 17, 37, 39, 42, 53, 55, 62, 99, 300, 300 and 400 frames, starts mostly not on a keyframe (sources have a keyframe every 60 frames; frame 1500 is one). 1405 frames, 46.83 s.

Notes on the checks:

- **Expected frames** for a constant-frame-rate source: frame `start + j` of the source for `j < count`. For the variable-frame-rate source: for each output slot `p`, the last source frame whose time rounded to the nearest slot is at most `p` (this is the rule of ffmpeg's `fps` filter, `round=near`). Wall-clock time is preserved.
- **Frame-accurate seeking off a keyframe** is covered by every clip whose start is not a multiple of 60, including a 1-frame clip (frame 400) and clips starting 1 frame after a keyframe (61, 1201). The fast seek (`-noaccurate_seek -ss <start - 1 s> -copyts`) lands on the keyframe before; `trim=start_pts` on the frame slot after `fps` is exact.
- **The join plays cleanly:** one packet per frame, every packet duration equal to one frame, pts in one regular sequence from 0, dts strictly increasing, a keyframe at the start of every piece. No gaps, no duplicates. The concat list with explicit `duration` lines and without them gave byte-identical files; keep the lines (the pieces' own durations are then never trusted).
- **Sync at the end:** the last beep of each track is within 0.06 ms of where it belongs; the largest error anywhere is 0.67 ms (on track 1 only, the same in every case, so a property of the generated source audio rather than of the cut). One frame is 33 ms.
- **All audio tracks survive:** two tracks in (stereo + mono), two tracks out, same layouts, same length as the video.
- **Audio priming delay:** handled by ffmpeg in both directions. Sources and outputs report audio `start_time 0.000000`; the MP4 muxer writes the AAC priming as an edit list. Do not add a manual offset.
- **Single pass** (one ffmpeg run, every clip a separate GPU-decoded input, `concat` filter) on the same 12-clip edit: also 0 mismatches, cuts right, regular pts, both beep tracks matched, 0.67 ms largest error (earlier run, `alt/singlepass_A_final.json`).

### Choice

- **cq 19** is the default for both codecs. cq 16 costs 39 % more size (H.264) for +0.35 VMAF on the hardest content. cq 22 drops H.264 to VMAF 95.2 on the camera half, where grain starts to visibly smooth. Screen content is VMAF 100 and SSIM 0.99999 at every setting.
- **p4** because p5 and p7 were no better (cq 19: VMAF 98.26 p5, 98.22 p4, 97.65 p7) and half the speed or less. p1 is 50 % faster but measurably worse (VMAF 97.94, PSNR 0.65 dB lower) and, for HEVC, 22 % larger.
- **By eye:** frames 3369 and 5136 of the source and of the cq 19 p4 / cq 22 p7 encodes were exported as PNGs (`.cache-dev/export/eye/`, earlier run). This run looked at them: the cq 19 p4 frame cannot be told from the source; the cq 19 p7 frame 3369 is visibly broken (blocks smeared across the picture), which is how the B-frame reference problem was found. The PNGs are downscaled to 960x540, so this is not a full-resolution viewing; repeat on the real recording.
- **"Visually lossless" default:** cq 19, p4, `-tune hq`, VBR with a ceiling at the level limit, 3 B-frames, spatial and temporal AQ, 32-frame lookahead, keyframe every 60 frames.

### Rate ceiling (care needed)

Without `-maxrate`, NVENC's VBR mode caps the bit rate by itself (earlier run, `bench/bench_implicit_cap.json`: H.264 cq 16 gave 61.3 Mb/s and cq 19 gave 59.5 Mb/s, nearly the same, because the encoder sat at its own cap on the camera half; camera VMAF 1 % was 81.3 at cq 16). So cq does not govern quality on hard scenes unless a ceiling is set. The recommended commands set it at the level limit: H.264 High@5.1 `-maxrate 240M -bufsize 300M`, HEVC High tier@5.1 `-maxrate 160M -bufsize 160M -tier high`. The highest rate actually used was 143 Mb/s (H.264 camera half).

### 10-bit and HDR

- HEVC Main10 in, HEVC Main10 out: `pix_fmt yuv420p10le`, profile Main 10, `hvc1` tag.
- HDR signalling survives with no extra options: `color_primaries bt2020`, `color_transfer smpte2084`, `color_space bt2020nc`, `color_range tv` on the stream, and Mastering display and Content light level metadata on **all 1405 frames** of the 12-clip export (`ffprobe ... frame_side_data`, this run). NVENC takes them from the decoded frames.
- BT.709 tags survive on the H.264 outputs (`bt709` primaries, transfer and matrix, `tv` range).
- **`h264_nvenc` cannot encode 10-bit** on this GPU: `Provided device doesn't support required NVENC features` (this run, a 10-bit source into `h264_nvenc`). The player spike found that H.264 High10 sources exist and play. For such a source the export must either use `hevc_nvenc` Main10 (keeps the bit depth, changes the codec family) or convert to 8-bit; spec 2.4.8 says "H.264 stays H.264", so this needs a decision. Not tested further.

### Model

From the piece times of the exactness runs (this run, idle GPU, least-squares fit, largest residual 0.2 s):

- **Per piece:** `1.05 s + 8.3 ms x frames` for 4K H.264 (12 pieces); `0.98 s + 8.1 ms x frames` for 4K H.264 from the variable-frame-rate source; `0.58 s + 2.2 ms x frames` for 1080p. The per-frame part scales with pixel count (1080p is a quarter of 4K: 2.2 against 8.3 ms). HEVC Main10 4K: 7.9 ms per frame on a whole-file encode (42.4 s for 5400 frames).
- **Audio:** about 0.05 s per clip at 12 clips (0.5 s); 102.6 s for 400 clips with the script form (earlier run, `audio/audio_scale.json`), so about **0.25 s per clip** at real clip counts. It uses the processor only and can run while the GPU encodes the pieces (not measured together).
- **Join:** writes the whole file once more: 1.3 s for 351 MB, 5.5 to 8.8 s for 1.2 to 1.6 GB, about **200 MB/s** on this disk.

So, for the Export dialog (`ExportPlan.estimatedSeconds`, `estimatedBytes`):

```
frames      = sequence duration x frame rate
pixel_scale = width x height / (3840 x 2160)
video_s     = clips x 0.5  + frames x 0.0075 x pixel_scale        (two pieces at a time, recommended)
            (= clips x 1.05 + frames x 0.0083 x pixel_scale             one piece at a time)
audio_s     = clips x 0.25                                        (0 if run alongside the video)
join_s      = estimatedBytes / 200e6
estimatedSeconds = video_s + audio_s + join_s

estimatedBytes = duration_s x (video_bytes_per_s + 32000 x audioTracks)
video_bytes_per_s = min(ceiling, ratio x source video bit rate) / 8
    ratio:   H.264 1.17 (R264: 1622 MB out of 1384 MB in), HEVC 3.15 (R265: 790 of 251 MB)
    ceiling: 240e6 (H.264), 160e6 (HEVC) bits per second
```

- Bytes per second of output at the chosen setting on the test source: **9.0 MB/s** H.264 (540.8 MB/min), **4.4 MB/s** HEVC (263.5 MB/min), plus 32 kB/s per audio track. These are worst-case-content figures; the ratio form adapts to the real source.
- Seconds of work per second of output: **0.23 to 0.25 s** for long clips (7.5 to 8.3 ms per 4K frame at 30 fps), plus 0.5 s per clip two at a time (1.05 s one at a time). The two-at-a-time coefficients are a fit to the four two-at-a-time runs above (it overestimates the HEVC 60-clip run, 57 s against 35 s measured); they are an estimate to refine on the real recording.
- **Worked example:** a 41-minute 4K30 H.264 result made of 300 clips (73,800 frames). Two at a time: 300 x 0.5 + 73,800 x 7.5 ms = 704 s, audio alongside (about 75 s, hidden), join about 22 GB at 200 MB/s = 110 s: **about 13.5 minutes**. One at a time: 315 + 613 + 75 + 110 s = about 18.5 minutes. Spec 2.4.8 and 2.6 say 7 to 10 minutes; that is not reached at this quality on this content. The output size here is the synthetic worst case; a real recording at a lower bit rate shortens only the join.
- **Disk space:** the pieces and the joined file exist together until the pieces are deleted, so the free-space check needs **2 x estimatedBytes** at the destination's drive if the pieces' temporary folder is on the same drive.
- **Progress** is exact: frames encoded so far over total frames (each piece's frame count is known); re-estimate the remaining time from the measured rate after the first pieces.


What varies per clip, and the rules:

- `-ss` = max(0, start_frame / fps - 1 s), written with 6 decimals; `-noaccurate_seek -copyts` so timestamps stay those of the source.
- `fps=<num>/<den>` = the sequence frame rate. After it the time base is 1/fps, so a frame's pts is its slot number: `trim=start_pts=<start_frame>:end_pts=<start_frame + count>` is exact. Do **not** use `trim=start=/end=` in seconds: `end` also drops the frame whose display interval crosses it (earlier run found this lost the last frame of every clip).
- `-frames:v <count>` as a second guard; `-fps_mode passthrough` so the muxer does not duplicate or drop frames.
- `-video_track_timescale` / `-movie_timescale`: 512 x fps for integer rates up to 127 fps (30 -> 15360, 25 -> 12800, 60 -> 30720), the rate's numerator for fractional rates (30000/1001 -> 30000), 90000 otherwise. Every piece and the join must use the same value, so every frame duration is a whole number of ticks.
- `-map_metadata -1` drops source tags (creation time etc.); colour tags and HDR metadata are stream properties and are not affected.
- H.264: `-profile:v high -b_ref_mode disabled -maxrate 240M -bufsize 300M`. HEVC: `-profile:v main10` for a 10-bit source, `main` for 8-bit, `-tier high -tag:v hvc1 -b_ref_mode middle -maxrate 160M -bufsize 160M`.
- B-frames at piece boundaries: each piece is a separate encode, so it starts with an IDR frame and its GOPs are closed; no B-frame refers across a join. The joined file's dts are strictly increasing and its pts regular (checked above).

## Things that needed care

- **H.264 B-frame references** (`-b_ref_mode middle`) make streams that libavcodec and NVDEC decode differently: `-b_ref_mode disabled` for H.264. Found by `decode_check.py`; reproduced deterministically (NVENC output for the same input is byte-identical between runs: 1,415,213,826 bytes both times), but a 300-frame piece of the same region did not show it, so a short test cannot prove a setting safe; check full-length encodes.
- **Rate ceiling**: set `-maxrate`/`-bufsize`, or cq stops governing hard scenes (above).
- **Trim by frame slot, not by seconds** (above).
- **Same timescale everywhere** (above). The concat demuxer then gives one regular pts sequence.
- **Audio from cumulative frame counts**, not per clip rounding (above).
- **Command-line length** for the audio graph (above).
- **Variable frame rate**: the `fps` filter rounds each source frame to the nearest slot and repeats or drops to fill the grid; the expected frames follow that rule (checked).
- **NVDEC output read back to the processor was not always repeatable.** In this run 24 decodes of finished files went through `-hwaccel cuda -hwaccel_output_format cuda` + `hwdownload`: 3 differed from the processor decode in 1 frame each (a different frame each time, never twice the same), and 1 (run while two other GPU decodes and an NVENC encode were running) in 513 frames; the other 20 matched exactly. 7 decodes through `-hwaccel cuda` without `-hwaccel_output_format` all matched, as did repeated processor decodes. Not reproduced on demand, so the cause is unknown (driver, other programs on the GPU, or the readback path), and it is not known whether it can also hit the decode inside an export piece. Every exported file in this spike passed the frame-marker check, but markers do not catch a damaged picture. The export module's integration test should keep a full-file decode comparison (`decode_check.py` does it) on its fixture; if it ever fails there, try `-hwaccel cuda` without `-hwaccel_output_format cuda` in the piece command (untested for speed).
- **10-bit H.264 sources** cannot be encoded by `h264_nvenc` (above).
